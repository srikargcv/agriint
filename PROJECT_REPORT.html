AgriNexus: An Ecosystem Intelligence Prototype for Agricultural Trade

A Project Report  ·  Agri-Tech / Supply Chain Intelligence

Abstract

Between the farm gate and the buyer, agricultural supply chains lose value through poor matching of produce to buyers, prices detached from market realities, routes that ignore spoilage, and fraud caught too late. AgriNexus is a working prototype of an ecosystem intelligence layer that automates four decisions — matching (which buyer gets which lot), pricing (whether an offer is fair), logistics (how to move produce without spoiling it), and trust & safety (scoring reliability and detecting fraud). The four modules cooperate through a single shared event stream: every step of every decision is published as a typed event, so the modules stay independent, every decision is traceable, and the whole pipeline can be watched live. A clean web interface presents each decision in plain language and allows arbitrary test scenarios to be submitted, making the system demonstrable to technical and non-technical reviewers alike. The implementation uses only the Python standard library with a small sample dataset, which keeps the project portable, readable, and easy to evaluate end-to-end.

Keywords: matching engine, fair price banding, route optimisation, spoilage risk, trust scoring, fraud detection, event-driven design.

1. Introduction

Agri-commerce platforms connect thousands of farmers with retail, restaurant (HoReCa), and export buyers. Without intelligence tooling, lots are matched opportunistically — a buyer is found who happens to want a crop, with little regard for whether the price, freshness, distance, or the farmer's reliability make the pairing a good one. Farmers rarely know the prevailing market rate at nearby market yards, so offers can drift far from fair in either direction, and the farmer — the weakest party in the chain — usually absorbs the loss. Routes are planned by distance alone even when the cargo is perishable and the day is hot. And anomalies such as weight disappearing between pickup and delivery, serial order cancellations, or groups of traders selling in circles to fake volume are typically caught only after money has moved.

The objective of this project was to design and build a compact, working prototype of an intelligence layer that addresses all four problems together, under two design constraints. First, every decision must be explainable: a reviewer should be able to see not just what the system decided but why — which factors pushed a match up or down, what market rate a price verdict was based on, why a particular vehicle was chosen. Second, the modules must be independent: they communicate only through a shared, typed event stream, so each module can be developed, tested, and later replaced or upgraded on its own without touching the others. This mirrors how real platforms evolve — algorithms improve module by module, not in one risky rewrite.

2. System Overview

MatchingWhich buyer should get which farmer's lot?
PricingIs the offered price fair to the farmer?
LogisticsWhat is the cheapest trip that keeps produce fresh?
Trust & FraudCan this actor be trusted — and is anything suspicious?

Data flows in one direction through the pipeline: farmer lots (crop, quantity, grade, freshness, location, asking price) and buyer orders (crop, quantity, maximum price, delivery hub, minimum trust requirement) enter the system; the matching engine pairs them subject to quality gates; each confirmed pair is then priced, routed, and checked for trust and fraud; and every step — match, price band, route plan, trust update, ledger entry, fraud flag — is published as an event on the shared stream. Because modules never read each other's internal state directly, the stream is also the system's audit trail: the dashboard's activity feed shows everything that happened, in order, and any integration problem appears immediately as a missing or malformed event.

The web dashboard is the system's public face. It presents the four modules as stations that can each be run individually on the sample dataset, plus a "run the full pipeline" action, plus a test-scenario form where a reviewer enters a lot and an order and receives a plain-language verdict from all four modules at once. All numbers are shown with their meaning — a spoil chance as a percentage, a price verdict as FAIR or UNFAIR with the market rate beside it — rather than as raw scores.

3. Module Summaries

Matching. Every farmer-lot/buyer-order pair is scored on five factors — price fit, freshness, distance, trust, and quantity fit — with the importance of each factor depending on the buyer type. A restaurant group weights freshness highest, since a wilting delivery hurts its business directly; an exporter weights trust highest, since its reputation rides on reliable partners; a retail cooperative sits between the two. Freshness is read from the lot's grade and its description; distance uses real coordinates between the farm and the buyer's delivery hub; trust is the farmer's current rolling score. Pairs below a quality threshold, or below the buyer's stated minimum trust requirement, are rejected outright — the system prefers no match to a bad one, which protects both sides.

Pricing. Each crop and district has a market reference rate drawn from published mandi (market yard) prices. The engine builds a fair band a little above and below that rate, adjusted for the lot's grade, the cost of moving the produce, and a small platform commission, so the farmer sees both the gross range and what lands in their hands. Offers inside the band are marked FAIR; offers outside trigger alerts in either direction — protecting farmers from lowball offers and buyers from gouging. A five-day price forecast accompanies every check, and a plain-language explanation is generated for the farmer ("today's market rate for your crop in your district is…, your fair range is…, prices are rising/softening"). This explanation slot is where an AI language model can later take over the wording without changing anything else.

Logistics. A typed vehicle fleet — small vans for light loads, pickup trucks for village-to-hub runs, mini trucks for mid-size loads, a 16-ton truck for bulk grain, and two refrigerated options — is matched to each load. Routes are planned to minimise not just kilometres but a spoilage-weighted cost: the longer a perishable sits on the road and the hotter the day, the more "expensive" that route becomes, so the planner naturally prefers trips that keep produce alive. Perishable loads in hot weather automatically receive refrigerated vehicles. Every trip is reported with its vehicle, pickups, distance, time on the road, and an explicit spoilage chance; trips whose risk crosses a safety line are flagged for re-planning before dispatch.

Trust & Fraud. A rolling trust score for each farmer rises with good deliveries and decays when problems occur, and feeds back into matching — trustworthy farmers unlock the buyers who demand reliability. Independently, every lot carries a tamper-evident record of its journey from sowing through harvest, pickup, cold storage, and delivery; each stage is sealed against the previous one, so any later edit to history breaks the seal and is detectable. This gives buyers provenance they can check rather than promises they must trust. Five fraud checks run continuously: weight that shrinks between pickup and delivery, prices wildly off the market, serial order cancellations, trucks reporting far from the claimed farm, and circular trading rings in which the same few traders sell to each other to fake volume. Flags escalate along a review path — flag for review, hold settlement, escalate to manual review.

4. Sample Results

Sample dataset: 8 farmers · 13 lots · 16 orders · 12 crops · 6 vehicle types
Matching: 9 confirmed pairs on the sample dataset; the best match paired a cotton lot from a Nellore farmer with an export order at a score of 0.91, with distance and quantity fit as the strongest factors.
Pricing: the majority of offers landed FAIR; asks above their fair band were flagged — for example A-grade cotton quoted above its computed range was marked UNFAIR and accompanied by an explanatory message stating the market rate, the fair range, and the net amount after fees.
Logistics: perishable loads on hot days were automatically assigned refrigerated trucks; a representative trip report reads "a refrigerated truck makes 1 pickup in Guntur — 89.7 km, about 2.3 hours on the road; chance of produce spoiling: 8%; fine," while an extreme long-distance perishable trip (over 1,200 km) pushed the spoil chance to 100% and triggered the re-planning flag.
Trust & Fraud: the demo lot's five-stage journey record re-verified intact; a farmer's trust climbed from 0.80 to 0.88 across three good deliveries; and all five fraud checks were demonstrated firing on injected test cases, concluding with a settlement hold.

The dashboard's test-scenario form extends these results to arbitrary inputs. Varying one field at a time makes the models' behaviour visible: raising the asking price above the buyer's maximum erodes the price-fit factor until the match flips to rejected; choosing a far-away delivery district raises both the distance penalty and the spoilage risk; forcing a non-refrigerated vehicle onto a long, hot, perishable run pushes the spoil chance upward and changes the trip's status; and entering mismatched pickup and delivery weights, or a GPS point far from the farm, produces the corresponding fraud warning in the verdict.

5. Testing

Testing followed two tracks. Unit-level tests exercised the matching engine's edge cases — a no-match case, threshold rejection, and the minimum-trust gate — confirming that each protection fires exactly when it should. System-level tests ran the full pipeline and the custom-scenario form through the browser interface, checking that every module renders correct results and that every step appears on the activity feed.

6. Limitations and Future Scope

The prototype deliberately trades scale for clarity. It uses a small sample dataset rather than live market feeds; its matching and routing algorithms are simple, explainable heuristics rather than globally optimal solvers; event history is held for the session rather than persisted to a database; and the web interface is intended for local, trusted use without authentication. Farmer-facing explanations are template-generated, with a clearly marked place where an AI language model can take over the wording later.

Each limitation has a natural upgrade path. Live market rates can replace the sample rates through government price APIs. Matching can move to a globally optimal assignment algorithm, and routing to a full vehicle-routing optimiser with time windows, both behind the same module interfaces. The event stream can be backed by a durable message log for analytics and audit. Authentication and role-based access can be added ahead of any multi-user deployment. And the fraud layer can grow graph analytics over the complete trader network as real transaction history accumulates.

7. Conclusion

AgriNexus shows that the four intelligence problems of an agri-commerce platform — matching, pricing, logistics, and trust — can be unified behind a single event contract and built as small, explainable, independently testable modules. The resulting prototype is fully working and demonstrable through its web interface; every decision a reviewer sees can be traced back to the factors and events that produced it. Just as importantly, the architecture anticipates its own growth: stronger algorithms, live data feeds, persistence, and AI- drafted explanations can each be adopted module by module, without reworking the system — which is precisely how production platforms evolve.
